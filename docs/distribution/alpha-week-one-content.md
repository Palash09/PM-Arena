## Reusable reply drafts

Use these as starting points only. Tailor the first sentence to the actual post and never attach a product link unless the community permits it and the author asks for a relevant resource.

**Structured thinking under pressure**

One practical test is to force the recommendation into five lines before the meeting: user/problem, evidence, decision, rejected alternative, and success metric. The writing is useful because it exposes the missing premise before someone challenges it live. Which of those five is hardest in your current environment?

**Frameworks versus judgment**

RICE or impact/effort can organize inputs, but they cannot decide the strategic weight assigned to those inputs. I would make the disagreement explicit: are people contesting the evidence, the confidence, or the objective itself? Each needs a different conversation.

**Roadmap overload**

If every initiative is critical, I would separate commitment risk from opportunity value. Fixed obligations, enabling dependencies, and reversible bets should not compete in one undifferentiated score. Which item creates the largest irreversible cost if delayed?

**Product sense**

Product sense becomes easier to coach when the decision is observable. Ask the PM to predict user behavior, choose a tradeoff, and state what evidence would falsify the prediction. That creates a feedback loop instead of treating taste as an innate trait.

**Strategy definition**

The most useful definition I have found is that strategy is a coherent choice about where to play and how to win under constraints. A roadmap can express that choice, but it cannot substitute for it. What constraint is your current strategy explicitly accepting?

**Growth loops**

I would distinguish a channel from a loop. A channel creates an acquisition event; a loop makes one user's value creation produce the next input. If the loop disappears when paid traffic stops, it is probably a tactic rather than a product growth system.

**PM versus program work**

Coordination is not automatically non-product work. The test is whether the work changes a product outcome or only reports delivery state. The danger is spending so much time transmitting status that no one owns the actual decision quality.

**Aspiring PM practice**

Case studies are stronger when they show a rejected alternative and a measurable outcome, not only a polished solution. Hiring teams need evidence that you can make tradeoffs, not just that you know the vocabulary.

**When someone asks for the product**

## Reddit draft: r/SideProject

**Title:** I turned product-management podcast decisions into a three-minute game. Where does the simulation lose credibility?

**Body:**

I have been building Product Decision League, a mobile-first decision game for product managers and people trying to move into product.

The problem I am testing: PM content is easy to consume but difficult to apply under pressure. Instead of searching a transcript, the player gets a real company situation, chooses an option, defends the tradeoff, and then sees the short attributed reveal from the product leader who faced it.

For the alpha I deliberately kept the loop small:

- Three flagship challenges: strategy, prioritization, and growth
- No login required for the public challenge
- A historical choice is preferred, but a well-reasoned alternative can still score well
- Signed-in progress is saved for a second scenario
- Stylized leader art and short excerpts identify the source without reproducing long transcript passages

The part I am least certain about is credibility. Does the scenario provide enough context to make the choice meaningful? Does the score explain a real reasoning gap, or does it feel like arbitrary AI judgment?

If you try one three-minute scenario, I would value an answer to one question:

**What felt least credible or most confusing?**

Tech stack for anyone interested: Next.js, TypeScript, Postgres/Prisma, Anthropic for bounded coaching, and rules-based fallback scoring.
